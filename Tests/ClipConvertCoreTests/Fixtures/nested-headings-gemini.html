Historians summarize the long-term underlying causes of World War I using the acronym M-A-I-N (Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism), alongside the immediate spark in Sarajevo.

1. Militarism

  • Naval Arms Race:

  • Britain and Germany engaged in a costly competition to build modern battleships (Dreadnoughts), heightening mutual suspicion.

  • Massive Standing Armies:

  • Continental European powers implemented mandatory conscription, expanding standing armies to unprecedented sizes.

  • Rigid War Plans:

  • Military leaders developed strict mobilization schedules—such as Germany’s Schlieffen Plan—that required immediate, automated offensive action once troops were mobilized.

2. Alliances

  • Triple Entente:

  • A defense diplomatic framework connecting Great Britain, France, and Russia to counter German dominance.

  • Triple Alliance:

  • A mutual defense agreement linking Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy (though Italy initially remained neutral in 1914).

  • Entangling Guarantees:

  • Secret treaties and localized defense pacts meant that a conflict between any two nations would trigger a chain reaction involving all major European powers.

3. Imperialism

  • Scramble for Africa:

  • European empires clashed over colonial territories, resource extraction, and global trade routes throughout the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

  • Moroccan Crises (1005 & 1911):

  • Repeated German challenges to French control over Morocco created severe diplomatic friction and strengthened Anglo-French ties.

  • Declining Ottoman Influence:

  • As Ottoman power faded in the Balkans, competing European empires sought to fill the power vacuum.

4. Nationalism

  • Pan-Slavism:

  • Russian support for Slavic independence in the Balkans fueled tensions with the multi-ethnic Austro-Hungarian Empire.

  • Balkan Instability (“Powder Keg of Europe”):

  • Small Balkan states fought wars for territorial expansion, raising national pride and regional volatility.

  • French Revanchism:

  • Deep-seated resentment in France over the loss of Alsace-Lorraine to Germany during the Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871).

5. The Immediate Catalyst (The Spark)

  • Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand:

  • On June 28, 1914, Bosnian-Serb nationalist Gavrilo Princip assassinated the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne in Sarajevo.

  • The July Crisis:

  • Austria-Hungary issued a severe ultimatum to Serbia with Germany’s backing (“blank check”).

  • Chain Reaction:

  • Serbia’s rejection of key terms led Austria-Hungary to declare war, triggering Russian mobilization and activating the alliance network within weeks.
